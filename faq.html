Common questions

Asked and answered.

The questions we hear most on the phone, answered properly. If yours is not here, it is probably in the knowledge centre — or on the other end of the line.

Which stitching wire is best for corrugated box manufacturing?

For dry-warehoused cartons consumed within a few weeks, galvanised (G.I.) stitching wire is the standard choice and the lowest cost per pin. For humid storage, monsoon transit or coastal warehousing, rust resistant or Power G.I. stitching wire carries a heavier zinc deposit that delays the onset of surface rust. For export cartons, pharmaceutical packaging and any box where a rust mark means rejection, pure brass stitching wire is specified — it contains no mild steel and therefore cannot rust at all.

What is the difference between G.I. stitching wire and brass stitching wire?

G.I. stitching wire is a mild steel core with a zinc coating applied by electroplating. The protection is a surface layer, so once it is scratched or worn through, the steel underneath can rust. Brass stitching wire is drawn from solid copper-zinc alloy with no mild steel anywhere in the section — there is no substrate to corrode and no coating to fail.

Brass costs several times more per kilogram. But a single stitch consumes only around 45 mm of wire, so the difference usually works out to a fraction of one percent of the value of the carton it closes. Run the numbers for your own box →

How do I choose the correct stitching wire gauge?

Gauge is set by two things: the board you are stitching, and the head you are stitching it with. Heavier flat sections — 20 to 22 gauge — suit multi-wall and heavy-duty corrugated. 23 to 24 gauge covers most single and double wall carton work. 25 to 26 gauge is used for light board, book stitching and saddle stitching.

Always confirm the gauge range your head is rated for before changing, because feed and clinch are set to a specific section. The machine finder lists the range for the common makes.

Why does stitching wire break during operation?

In our experience the causes, in order of frequency: inconsistent flattening, so thickness varies along the length and the head cannot hold a steady feed; excessive coating build-up creating drag in the wire guide; incorrect gauge for the head; a worn or misaligned cutter and former; and poorly wound spools that produce cross-overs and snatching.

Only the first two are the wire's fault. Wire that is gauged lot by lot for thickness, width and tensile consistency will not break for reasons that originate in the wire.

How should stitching wire be stored?

Keep spools in their original wrapping, on pallets rather than on the floor, in a dry covered area away from external walls. Avoid rooms where the temperature swings sharply — condensation forming on cold metal is the main cause of storage rust in galvanised wire, not humidity alone. First in, first out.

Brass and copper wire have no storage rust risk and can be held indefinitely.

Do you supply export orders and what are your packing standards?

Yes. Export consignments are supplied on sealed reels with VCI wrapping where the grade calls for it, palletised and shrink-wrapped, with mill test certificates and packing lists issued against reel numbers. Container condensation over a long sea leg is the single biggest cause of rust claims on stitched cartons, which is why we generally recommend brass or copper for any box crossing the equator.
